\section{Related literature and organization}
\whenintegrated{\label{sec:literature}}

\whenintegrated{\label{related-literature}}\par\subsection{Related literature}
\whenintegrated{\label{sec:relatedlit}} % integrated doc: SST for labels

Our paper builds on, and brings together, four strands of the sovereign-debt literature: work on the composition of debt holders and default risk; the financial-repression literature on domestic debt bias; the sovereign--bank ``doom loop''; and the classical literature on the optimal level of government debt. Existing work addresses pieces of the picture: the default literature studies the choice between domestic and foreign creditors; the financial-repression literature explains why governments push debt onto banks; and the home-bias literature documents preferences for domestic sovereign debt. Our contribution is to shift the focus from the \emph{level} of debt to the \emph{allocation} across holder types and to provide a structural framework that links observable financial-sector heterogeneity to the cross-country pattern of debt composition.

\whenintegrated{\label{lit-composition}}
\subsubsection{Government debt composition and default risk}
A first strand studies how sovereigns choose between domestic and foreign creditors when default is selective. \citet{Broneretal2010} show that secondary-market trading between foreigners and domestic residents can sustain cross-border lending even when sovereigns would otherwise prefer to default on foreigners. \citet{GennaioliMartinRossi2014} model the interaction between sovereign default and domestic financial development, showing how the holdings of sovereign debt by domestic banks determine the government's default incentives. Empirically, \citet{ArslanalpTsuda2014} construct a database that tracks global demand for advanced-economy sovereign debt by holder type, and \citet{FangHardyLewis2025} document the patterns and macroeconomic consequences of sovereign-debt holdings across countries. Our paper builds on this empirical infrastructure but goes further by providing a unified framework for the \emph{three-way} allocation---domestic non-banks, foreigners, and banks---that the data actually display.

\whenintegrated{\label{lit-repression}}
\subsubsection{Financial repression and domestic debt bias}
A second strand studies why governments push debt onto the domestic banking sector. \citet{ReinhartSbrancia2015} provide historical evidence that financial repression---a mix of interest-rate ceilings, reserve requirements and captive domestic audiences for government debt---has been a quantitatively important mechanism through which governments liquidate their debt burdens. \citet{ChariDovisKehoe2020} rationalize these policies in a Ramsey framework and show that forcing banks to hold sovereign debt can be part of an optimal taxation scheme when the government lacks commitment. Most directly related to our paper, \citet{Jeanne2025} models optimal financial repression as a sequential process: below a debt threshold banks act as marginal buyers (balance-sheet repression), and above it the government extracts quasi-fiscal revenue from the banking sector. That paper establishes why banks must absorb debt beyond a certain level, but treats the non-bank sector as a single passive residual. We take the balance-sheet-repression mechanism as a starting point and ask a complementary question: \emph{among the non-bank creditors, how does debt distribute between domestic non-banks and foreigners, and what determines the cross-country heterogeneity in this allocation?}

\whenintegrated{\label{lit-doomloop}}
\subsubsection{Sovereign--bank doom loop}
A third strand emphasizes the two-way feedback between sovereign risk and bank balance sheets. \citet{BrunnermeierEtAl2016} formalize the ``diabolic loop'' between weak sovereigns and weak banks and propose safe-asset designs that would break it. \citet{FarhiTirole2018} develop a general-equilibrium model in which bank bailouts and sovereign distress reinforce each other, generating a genuine doom loop. This literature motivates a central premise of our framework: placing marginal debt in the banking sector is not costless, because bank-held sovereign debt distorts bank balance sheets and amplifies macro-financial feedbacks. Our model captures this by treating bank absorption as the \emph{residual} margin with a convex welfare cost, rather than as a perfectly elastic funding source.

\whenintegrated{\label{lit-optimaldebt}}
\subsubsection{Optimal government debt level}
A fourth strand provides a rationale for why non-banks hold government debt in the first place. \citet{AiyagariMcGrattan1998} show that in an incomplete-markets setting, households value government bonds as a self-insurance instrument against uninsured idiosyncratic income risk, pinning down an optimal positive quantity of public debt. This provides a microeconomic rationale for a downward-sloping, capacity-bounded non-bank demand curve. That framework, however, is closed-economy and has no sovereign default, so it leaves unaddressed the two margins at the center of our paper: sovereign default risk and the role of foreign investors. Our model does not micro-found the non-bank demand curve in the Aiyagari--McGrattan sense; instead, we posit a reduced-form demand $d_h = S_h \cdot g(r_h)$ consistent with that tradition, and focus on how non-bank demand, a foreign no-default ceiling, and bank residual absorption jointly determine the allocation of sovereign debt.

\whenintegrated{\label{organization}}\par\subsection{Organization}
\whenintegrated{\label{sec:org}} % integrated doc: SST for labels

The paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:model} presents the three-sector cost-minimization model, in which the government allocates debt across domestic non-banks with country-specific capacity, foreigners with a uniform no-default ceiling, and banks as the residual absorber with convex financial-repression costs, and derives the resulting equilibrium predictions for debt composition. Section~\ref{sec:empirical} takes the model to the data: we describe the panel of advanced-economy debt-composition data and structural sector-size variables, implement a two-stage estimator that identifies structural sector sizes in a first stage and the cross-country allocation of debt in a second stage, and document the resulting heterogeneous pecking order and concavity in debt absorption. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
