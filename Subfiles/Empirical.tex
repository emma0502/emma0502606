\section{Empirical Analysis}
\whenintegrated{\label{sec:empirical}}

We bring the cost-minimizing allocation model of Section~\ref{sec:model}
to a panel of 32 advanced economies over 1989--2021. The empirical
strategy is a two-stage estimator: a first stage recovers each
country's structural sector size $\hat\delta_{ij}$, and a second stage
uses these as interaction variables to estimate the slope and
threshold behaviour of debt allocation.

%-------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Data and sector-size variables}
\label{sec:data}
%-------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Panel.}
The unit of observation is a country--year. The sample covers $32$
advanced economies over $1989$--$2021$, for a balanced maximum of
$32\times 33 = 1{,}056$ country--year observations. Coverage is
unbalanced: it thickens after $\sim\!2000$ and is full from $2007$
onward. Cyprus is dropped from non-bank specifications because the
underlying non-bank size variable is missing for all years; the
headline sample of $N=780$ observations refers to the central-bank and
private-bank Stage~2 regressions.

\paragraph{Variables.}
We use two data sources.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Debt holdings.} \citet{ArslanalpTsuda2014}, sovereign
        debt by creditor sector. We observe holdings by three domestic
        sectors---non-banks ($d_h$), central bank ($d_{cb}$), and
        private banks ($d_{pb}$). Foreign holdings $d_f$ are treated
        as the accounting residual:
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:foreign-residual}
          d_{f,it}=D_{it}-d_{h,it}-d_{cb,it}-d_{pb,it}.
        \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Sector-size proxies.} World Bank,
        \emph{Global Financial Development Database} (GFDD). We
        construct a country--year proxy $s_{j,it}$ for the structural
        size $S_j$ of each domestic sector $j\in\{h,cb,pb\}$:
        \begin{align*}
          s_{h,it}  &= \tfrac{\text{Pension fund assets}_{it}+\text{Insurance assets}_{it}}{\text{GDP}_{it}}
                       && \to\; S_h\;(\text{non-bank capacity}) \\
          s_{pb,it} &= \tfrac{\text{Bank deposit funding}_{it}}{\text{GDP}_{it}}
                       && \to\; S_{pb}\;(\text{private-bank size}) \\
          s_{cb,it} &= \tfrac{\text{Central bank assets}_{it}}{\text{GDP}_{it}}
                       && \to\; S_{cb}\;(\text{central-bank size}).
        \end{align*}
\end{itemize}
No country-specific size proxy is available for foreign investors,
since foreign creditors are global balance sheets rather than a local
sector we can size in GFDD. We therefore run \emph{no first-stage
regression for foreigners} and rely on \eqref{eq:foreign-residual} to
recover $d_{f,it}$.

%-------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Empirical strategy: two-stage estimator}
%-------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Threshold.}
$\bar D = 102.7\%$ of GDP, estimated from the data in \S6.3:
banking's marginal absorption accelerates sharply once $D_{it}$
crosses this level (Section~\ref{sec:motivation}).

\paragraph{Stage 1---structural sector size $\hat\delta_{ij}$.}
For each country $i$ and sector $j\in\{h,cb,pb\}$, regress sector
share on total debt with country and year fixed effects, allowing a
kink at $\bar D$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stage1}
s_{ijt}=\delta_{ij}+\delta_{jt}
       +\alpha_{1j}D_{it}
       +\alpha_{2j}\,\mathbf{1}\{D_{it}\!\ge\!\bar D\}\,(D_{it}-\bar D)
       +u_{ijt}.
\end{equation}
We read $\hat\delta_{ij}$, the country fixed effect, as the part of
country $i$'s sector-$j$ size that does not move with time, after
stripping out variation explained by $D_{it}$ and the kink.

\paragraph{Stage 2---debt allocation.}
The effective slope is a product of a baseline rate and a size
scaling, with a kink at $\bar D$. In its original factored form,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stage2-factored}
d^j_{it}
= \underbrace{\beta_j\!\left(1+\gamma_j\hat\delta_{ij}\right)}_{\text{baseline slope}} D_{it}
+ \mathbf{1}_{it}\,
  \underbrace{\eta_j\!\left(1+\rho_j\hat\delta_{ij}\right)}_{\text{post-threshold slope change}}
  (D_{it}-\bar D)
+ \varepsilon_{ijt}.
\end{equation}
Expanding the two products gives the estimating equation reported in
the tables:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stage2}
d^j_{it}=\beta_j D_{it}
        +\theta_j(\hat\delta_{ij}\!\cdot\! D_{it})
        +\mu_j\mathbf{1}_{it}(D_{it}-\bar D)
        +\nu_j\mathbf{1}_{it}(\hat\delta_{ij}\!\cdot\!(D_{it}-\bar D))
        +\varepsilon_{ijt},
\end{equation}
with $\theta_j=\beta_j\gamma_j$, $\mu_j=\eta_j$, and
$\nu_j=\eta_j\rho_j$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\beta_j$: baseline slope---sector $j$'s absorption per unit
        of $D$.
  \item $\theta_j$: \emph{size-scaling}---larger $\hat\delta_{ij}$
        implies steeper absorption.
  \item $\mu_j,\nu_j$: post-threshold slope change and its
        size-scaling.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Inference.}
Standard errors throughout the empirical section are two-way
cluster-robust at the country and year level, following
\citet{CameronGelbachMiller2011}. This is more conservative than
one-way country clustering and allows for arbitrary within-country
serial correlation \emph{and} arbitrary within-year cross-country
correlation in the residuals.

%-------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Estimating the high-debt threshold}
\label{sec:hansen-threshold}
%-------------------------------------------------------------

We estimate the threshold $\bar D$ from the data using a Hansen-style
threshold regression of total banking-sector holdings,
$\mathit{db}_{it}\equiv d_{cb,it}+d_{pb,it}$, on the level of total
debt $D_{it}$:
\[
  \mathit{db}_{it}
   = \alpha_1\, D_{it}\,\mathbf{1}\{D_{it}<\bar D\}
   + \alpha_2\, D_{it}\,\mathbf{1}\{D_{it}\ge\bar D\}
   + \xi_{it},
\]
with $\bar D$ chosen by least squares on a grid of candidate values.
Column~(1) of Table~\ref{tab:threshold} includes country fixed
effects, identifying $\bar D$ from within-country variation in debt
levels; column~(2) is the pooled cross-section.

The estimates split cleanly along the model's two-versus-one
taxonomy. For non-banks, neither $\alpha_{1h}$ nor $\alpha_{2h}$ is
statistically distinguishable from zero, so the size of the non-bank
sector does not move with total debt either below or above the
threshold. This is what we would expect under a \emph{hard} capacity
$\bar\omega S_h$ whose level evolves country by country but does not
respond to the sovereign's issuance choices. The two banking-sector
regressions tell the opposite story: $\alpha_{2j}$ is positive and
significant in both the central-bank and private-bank columns, so
banking-sector size \emph{grows} once $D_{it}$ crosses $\bar D$. That
growth is the empirical counterpart of banking's no-hard-ceiling
property in the model --- when non-banks and foreigners saturate, the
planner channels marginal debt onto the banking system and the
banking system itself expands to absorb it.

The Stage~2 estimates line up with three of the four model
predictions and refine the fourth. The baseline slopes $\beta_j$ are
positive and highly significant across all three sectors, confirming
that every domestic creditor absorbs some share of marginal issuance
--- the empirical counterpart of Proposition~\ref{prop:monotone}.
The non-bank slope scales further with structural size: $\theta_h>0$
at conventional significance, so countries with intrinsically larger
non-bank sectors absorb a larger fraction of any given $D$, exactly
the size-scaling channel of Proposition~\ref{prop:Sh}. Above the
threshold the picture becomes one of banking dominance. The combined
post-threshold slope $\mu_{cb}+\mu_{pb}\approx 0.82$ implies that,
once $D_{it}$ crosses $\bar D$, banking absorbs roughly $82$
percentage points more of each additional unit of debt than below;
the private-bank component ($\mu_{pb}=0.54$, $p=0.03$) is significant
at conventional levels and the central-bank component ($\mu_{cb}=0.28$,
$p=0.08$) at the $10\%$ level under two-way clustering. This is the
banking-takeover prediction of Section~\ref{sec:model} in
quantitative form.

A subtler pattern shows up in the heterogeneity of the central-bank
takeover. The interaction $\nu_{cb}$ is negative and significant,
meaning that the post-threshold acceleration is concentrated in
countries whose baseline central-bank balance sheet is \emph{small}:
economies with already-large central banks have less marginal
absorption to do once $D$ crosses $\bar D$. Read alongside the
model, this is the natural reflection of the planner's logic ---
central banks that are already large at low $D$ have already done
their share of crowding-in, leaving the post-threshold absorption to
fall on the smaller-balance-sheet countries.

\paragraph{Foreign debt residual.}
Using the fitted domestic components from \eqref{eq:stage2}, the
implied foreign residual tracks the data: correlation
$\approx 0.78$, slope $\approx 0.74$, $R^2\approx 0.61$, MAE
$\approx 8.8$. These diagnostics are essentially unchanged from a
$\bar D=100$ specification, since changing the kink location by a
few percentage points of GDP barely shifts the fitted values.

%-------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Concavity in debt absorption}
%-------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Specification.}
For sector $j\in\{h,f\}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:concavity}
d^j_{it}=\beta_j D_{it}+\kappa_j D_{it}^2
        +\theta_j(\hat\delta_h\!\cdot D_{it})+\varepsilon_{it},
\end{equation}
where $\theta_j$ is included only for the non-bank sector. Pooled and
country-FE estimates are reported separately, with and without
$\hat\delta_h\!\cdot D$.

The two capped sectors show concavity, but at different margins.
For non-banks the curvature term $\kappa_h$ is negative and
significant in the country-FE specification but moves toward zero
once we condition on the size interaction $\hat\delta_h\!\cdot D$,
so the within-country slowdown is driven by country-specific
non-bank capacity $S_h$ rather than by an absolute level of debt.
For foreigners, by contrast, $\kappa_f<0$ shows up only in the
pooled specification and not within country, which is exactly the
signature of a \emph{common} foreign ceiling $\bar D_f$ that is
shared across the panel: the curvature is identified by
cross-country variation in how close each economy sits to the same
underlying limit. Taken together these two findings deliver
Proposition~\ref{prop:concave}: the marginal absorption shares of
non-banks and foreigners both decline as $D$ grows, but the source
of concavity differs by sector --- country-specific $S_h$ for
non-banks, a common $\bar D_f$ for foreigners.
