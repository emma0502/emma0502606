\section{Conclusion}\whenintegrated{\label{conclusion}}
\whenintegrated{\label{sec:conclusion}} % integrated doc: SST for labels

This paper studies how governments allocate debt across creditor sectors.
The starting point is a simple empirical puzzle: countries with similar levels
of public debt often have very different creditor mixes. Some rely heavily on
domestic non-bank investors, others on foreign investors, and others on the
banking system. At the same time, the data show a sharp change at high debt
levels: once debt rises to around $100\%$ of GDP, the banking sector begins to
absorb a much larger share of additional government debt.

To explain these patterns, the paper develops a cost-minimizing model of
sovereign debt allocation. The government must place a given stock of debt and
chooses how much to allocate to domestic non-banks, foreign investors, and the
banking sector. The key distinction across sectors is their effective capacity.
Domestic non-bank capacity is country-specific and depends on the depth of the
local financial system. Foreign demand is limited by a common exposure ceiling.
The banking sector has no hard ceiling, but greater reliance on banks carries
increasing quasi-fiscal and financial-stability costs. This structure generates
a pecking-order logic: at low and moderate debt levels, non-banks and foreigners
absorb much of the debt; as these sectors become saturated, marginal debt
absorption shifts toward the banking system.

The empirical results support the main predictions of the model. Using a panel
of 32 advanced economies, the paper finds that countries with larger structural
non-bank sectors absorb more government debt through non-bank investors at a
given debt level. This size-scaling result is consistent with the model's
prediction that domestic financial structure shapes the creditor composition of
public debt. The results also show two distinct forms of concavity. Non-bank
absorption becomes concave within countries, consistent with country-specific
domestic capacity constraints. Foreign absorption is instead concave mainly
across countries, consistent with a common foreign-investor ceiling. Finally,
the estimated high-debt threshold is around $103\%$ of GDP. Above this threshold,
the combined marginal absorption of central banks and private banks rises
sharply, indicating a banking-sector takeover of marginal government debt.

These findings suggest that the fiscal consequences of high public debt depend
not only on the level of debt, but also on the structure of the investor base.
A country with a deep domestic non-bank sector can place more debt outside the
banking system and may therefore face a different form of fiscal pressure than a
country with a thinner non-bank investor base. Conversely, when non-bank and
foreign demand become saturated, additional debt is more likely to migrate
toward the banking sector, strengthening the sovereign--bank nexus and raising
the risk of financial repression or quasi-fiscal costs.
